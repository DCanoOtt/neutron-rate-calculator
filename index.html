1. What the page does

It folds the neutron fluence of a facility with a pointwise (continuous-energy) cross section from an ACE library. The result is the reaction rate of the sample as a function of neutron energy, its integral, and how that integral divides over energy decades. All rates are time-averaged and given in 1/s unless "per pulse" is stated.

2. Quick start

(1) Nuclear data: under Cross sections (ACE) press Choose folder… and select the folder that holds xsdir, or load one or more ACE files (in Chrome and Edge the page remembers the folder and offers it again next time). (2) Facility: choose the beam line, and change repetition rate and flight path if needed. (3) Target definition: the drawing shows, in beam order, the optional vacuum window + air in front of the target and the three layers of the target (front layer, sample and sample backing); the sample is always on, click another layer to switch it on, and double-click a layer to define it in its own window. The vacuum window (with an optional air gap) only attenuates the beam: choose a material, its thickness and the length of air in front of the target. (4) Sample: find the primary isotope, tick one or more reaction channels, add components if the sample is a compound, and enter mass or number of atoms and, if you want a thickness, the density; the beam spot radius is in the Target definition panel. The front layer and the backing have their own isotopes, reactions, mass, density and thickness. (5) Optionally apply the time-of-flight resolution function. (6) Read the three charts, the summary band under them and the tables further down. Everything recalculates as soon as a field changes. Use the Axis toggle above the charts to show energy or time of flight. (7) For a measurement, open the Experiment simulation window (see section 5).

3. Setting up a calculation (left panel)

Facility. The beam lines come from the fluence file (title, number of points, then energy–flux pairs; energy in MeV, flux in n/(cm²·s·ΔlnE)). The file read at start is Fluxes01.txt. Fluence file for all beam lines replaces the whole set with another file of the same layout. Replace this beam line's fluence pastes or loads a two-column table for the selected line only; choose its energy unit and whether the values are per ΔlnE, per MeV or per pulse. Repetition rate and Flight path start at the nominal values of the line; Reset to nominal restores them. The information list shows the pulse spacing, the fluence scale against the nominal setting and the pulse-overlap energy. The title of n_TOF-EAR2 says 20 m, but its real flight path is 19.55 m, and the page uses 19.55 m as the nominal value for it (in the flight path field, the fluence scaling and the time of flight).

Resolution (time of flight), in the Facility group. Switches the resolution function on. Pulse width starts at the value of the selected beam line (n_TOF: σ = 7 ns, shown as 16.484 ns FWHM; NFS 5.6 ns, DONES 5.6 ns, GELINA 1 ns); you can overwrite it, and it is remembered for each beam line until Reset to nominal. Added width adds other contributions (default 0). The select chooses whether those two widths are FWHM or σ. Target / moderator spread adds the contribution of the production target and moderator. Monte Carlo RF (default for EAR1 and EAR2) replaces the target spread by a resolution function. The EAR1 and EAR2 functions come from Monte Carlo histograms, parameterised with smoothed spline parameters so that statistical spikes and valleys of the histograms do not appear. The NEAR (6 m) function was not simulated: it was extrapolated from the EAR1 and EAR2 target-only functions (quantiles linear in ln L, with no extrapolation below 0.3 eV where the thermal shapes differ) and refitted, so treat it as indicative; for this reason the default for NEAR is Fixed path length ΔL = 40 cm, and the Monte Carlo RF can still be selected. For EAR1 and NEAR the proton pulse (σ = 7 ns, shown as FWHM) is added by the page. For EAR2 (19.55 m) it already contains the proton pulse (7 ns RMS) and the target and moderator, so the pulse width and ΔL fields are disabled and only Additional width still applies. For EAR1 (185 m, FLUKA) it contains the target and moderator only: the proton pulse is added as a Gaussian in time, with a default of 7 ns RMS (16.48 ns FWHM, shown in the pulse field in the units chosen below) that you can change. The flight path of the resolution function is used for the time-of-flight conversion and axis. The resolution function gives the distribution of the extra equivalent path length λ for each neutron energy, as a Gaussian convolved with two exponential tails, (1 − f2) EMG(μ, σ, τ1) + f2 EMG(μ, σ, τ2), with the parameters (EAR2: μ about 6 cm at low energy and 30 cm above 1 MeV; fast tail τ1 of 2 to 40 cm, slow tail τ2 of 40 to 130 cm; EAR1: edge at about 10 cm) interpolated in log E; for EAR1 below about 0.1 eV a third broad Gaussian around 150 cm describes thermal neutrons that leave the moderator late; a neutron of energy E arrives at t = (L + λ) / v. Each energy bin of the rate spectrum is spread over apparent times with this distribution, so the total rate is conserved. The distribution has a long tail towards later times (lower apparent energies) and, above about 10 MeV, towards negative λ. Fixed path length ΔL (default 0.5 cm for every beam line without a Monte Carlo RF) gives a time spread ΔL / v(E). The total resolution is the quadrature sum of the pulse width, the additional width (detector, electronics, moderator) and the fixed-path-length target spread; the line under the fields gives the resulting ΔE/E at 1 eV, 1 keV and 1 MeV. The total resolution is the quadrature sum of all parts. The flight path is the one in the beam line name. See section 6 for what is broadened.

Nuclear data panel (cross sections, ACE). The Nuclear data panel, directly below Setup, loads the cross-section library (and the decay data, see below); the isotope is chosen in the Sample panel (and in the layer panels). The long explanation of the folder dialog is also in the collapsible Info label under the buttons. A browser cannot open a path that is typed in. The path field is only a reminder (it is kept in this browser). The folder button opens a file dialog (on macOS press ⌘⇧G and paste the path). The page then looks for xsdir, reads the list of ASCII neutron tables (names such as 26056.80c) and finds each isotope's file by the name written in xsdir, in any subfolder of the chosen folder (a datapath= line in xsdir, or a folder in the file name, narrows the search when several files share a name). Only the isotope you pick is read, so a large library loads quickly. Without an xsdir, the page reads the headers of all ACE files in the folder. Single ACE files can be loaded with the second chooser. Type-1 ASCII files are supported, with the old 12-line header and the ACE 2.0.0 header; binary ACE is not. The status line below the buttons reports what was read, or the file name and the problem when something fails. Problems are reported, not hidden: a table listed in xsdir whose file is not in the folder is shown as file missing (greyed out in the list, not counted as data, and the status line gives how many); a table that cannot be read because the file is cut off or damaged (header numbers inconsistent, values that are not numbers, an energy grid that does not increase) is reported with the reason and marked damaged, and the other tables keep working. Without an xsdir the headers of all files are read, which for a folder with thousands of files takes some time (the status line shows the progress); an xsdir in the folder makes it instant. Files that cannot be read as ACE are counted, with a few names. The list of tables shows at most 800 entries, so type in the search box to narrow it.

Find isotope filters the list: use Au-197, 79197 or a full ZAID such as 26056.80c. If nothing matches, or the xsdir entry points to a file that is not in the folder, an error message says so.

Reaction channels. The list holds the MTR/SIG reactions of the table, plus total (MT 1), elastic (MT 2) and absorption (MT 101) from the main energy grid. MT 3 (non-elastic) is derived as total minus elastic. Tick one or more. You can also untick every reaction (Untick all, or one by one): an isotope without a ticked reaction contributes no rate but still shields the sample, and is listed as shielding only. If no reaction at all is ticked, in the primary isotope or in any component, the page shows a note instead of the plots. Reset reaction selection returns to a single reaction: radiative capture (MT 102), or fission (MT 18) if the table has no capture channel, and puts the result selectors back to their defaults.

Isotope chart. The Isotope chart button above Find isotope opens a floating chart of the nuclides (neutron number N across, proton number Z up). Colours give the main decay mode of the ground state: black stable, blue β⁻, light red EC / β⁺, yellow α, green spontaneous fission, orange proton emission, violet neutron emission, tan other. Isotopes that have a table in the loaded library are drawn in full colour with an outline; the others are faded. Use the wheel or the + / − buttons to zoom, or drag a rectangle (Zoom box) to zoom into a region; Move (or Shift+drag) drags the chart; double-click or Whole chart shows everything, and Selected centres on the current isotope. The title bar moves the window and its corner resizes it. The magic numbers 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82 and 126 are bracketed by thin dashed orange lines and labelled in orange on both axes. A click on an isotope with data selects it as the isotope of the page; Shift+click adds it to the compound. Isomer tables (metastable states, with ZAIDs such as 95642 for Am-242m in the MCNP numbering) are marked by a small triangle in the corner of the cell; a cell that has only an isomer table is also outlined. Alt+click selects the isomer instead of the ground state, and the isomer is listed in the information window. Tables that are missing or damaged are not counted as data. Moving the pointer over an isotope opens a small window with its name, Z, N, A, decay mode, half-life and the ZAIDs available. Decay data: NUBASE.

Target definition, layers and compounds. The target is made of up to three layers along the beam: front layer (for example a container wall), sample and sample backing. In front of the target you can optionally define a vacuum window and an air gap (one box, Vacuum window + air); they are not part of the target. The Target definition panel draws the window and the three layers (the sample is thicker). Click a layer in the drawing, or its tab below, to select it; clicking an off layer (or the window) switches it on and the × on a tab switches it off again (its isotopes are kept for when you switch it back on). The sample is always on. Double-click a layer in the drawing, or its box below, to open its own window, where you define the isotopes, the reactions to count and the mass, number of atoms, thickness and density (Esc or Close closes it); each box shows a one-line summary. Sample: the primary isotope plus the components you add with Add component form one homogeneous mixture; each component has its number of atoms per primary atom (real values allowed, for example 2 for the oxygen in UO₂), its own isotope filter, table selector and reactions to count. Front layer and backing have their own material, independent of the sample: a pure isotope or a compound. Add one component per isotope with its atoms per formula unit (for example 1 and 2 for a 1:2 compound); the layer has its own mass (mg), density (g/cm³) and thickness (µm): the mass fixes the number of atoms, and thickness and mass are linked through the density (default: the element density for a single element; for a compound enter it). Reset reaction selection unticks all reactions of a component and Remove deletes it. A component with no reaction ticked only takes part in the shielding; with reactions ticked, each one gets its own row in Rates by reaction. Loading another library clears the components (the vacuum window is kept and its tables are read again). Each layer shields with its own total cross sections (MT 1), and a layer is attenuated by the layers in front of it (the vacuum window and the air gap are applied to the fluence itself, see below): the front layer reduces the beam reaching the sample, the backing does not (it sees the beam after the sample). In the correction models the layers are included: the Monte Carlo follows scattered neutrons through the three layers of the target (backscatter from the backing and scatter from the front layer into the sample included), and the analytic second-collision term is an integral over the three layers. The layers are infinite slabs with elastic scattering only. Target and stack. The target is the assembly of up to three layers (front layer, sample and backing); the vacuum window and the air gap in front of it are not part of the target. In the experiment simulation Identical targets in the beam places N copies of this target one behind the other along the beam: every copy has its own front layer, sample and backing, the first sees the beam after the window and the air gap, and target k sees it attenuated by the k−1 targets before it; the window and the air gap are applied only once, not repeated. The Info labels in the panels repeat these rules.

Radioactivity of the layers and emission spectra. Optionally load decay data in the Decay data block of the Nuclear data panel: they are found automatically if the library folder has a sub-folder <library name>_decay; otherwise press Load decay data… and choose the folder with the ENDF/B-VIII.0 decay sublibrary (dec-*.endf) or with the single JEFF-4.0 decay file (ENDF-6 format, MF 8 / MT 457; both can be downloaded from the public sites given in the README); the page recognises which one it is, and if both are found you choose which one is used.

Show decay data (in the Nuclear data panel and in the experiment window) opens a window with a section for the vacuum window + air, the front layer, the sample, the backing, the whole target (front layer + sample + backing, when at least two layers are radioactive) and, if the experiment has several identical targets, all the targets together; sections without radioactive material are not shown, and neither are the spectra buttons of radiations that a section does not emit. Each section lists every radioactive isotope with its number of atoms, half-life, decay modes, activity (Bq) and the α, β⁻, β⁺ and γ particles emitted per second, plus the totals; it has buttons for the alpha, beta, gamma + X-ray and conversion-electron spectra, and the window has the save buttons (all spectra of all sections as PNG, PDF, ASCII or ROOT, and the tables as ASCII). The atoms of the window and the air need the beam spot radius.

Daughters and the time after the preparation. The window has the field Time since the source was prepared (number and unit). By default it is 7 half-lives of the initial isotope (the radioactive isotope with the highest activity), in the unit of its half-life; the button 7 half-lives returns to it after you change it. The daughters of every radioactive isotope are then calculated with the Bateman equations for that time, following the chain up to eight generations, with the branching ratios and the isomeric state of the daughter (for example Cs-137 gives Ba-137m with its 661.7 keV line); a daughter is listed with ↳ and the name of its parent, and its activity is not shown when it is below 10−6 of the activity of the initial isotope. The atoms of the initial isotope are those entered in the page, taken at the time of the measurement; the daughters start from zero at the preparation. If you empty the time field, the short-lived daughters are taken in equilibrium with their parent instead: a daughter that is shorter-lived than its parent and has a half-life up to the limit of the Nuclear data panel (automatic = a tenth of the half-life of the initial isotope, at most 30 d, unless you enter one) gets the activity Ap × branching ratio × λd/(λd − λp). The option Include short-lived daughters of that panel switches all daughters off. Time, unit and the daughter options are saved in the setup.

Spectra. The buttons α spectra, β⁻/β⁺ spectra, γ + X-ray spectra and Conversion e⁻ spectra open a window with the emission rate per energy bin of that section. The plot has a legend with one curve per isotope (the ten strongest, the rest lumped as Other) and the Total of all of them; click a legend entry to hide or show that curve. The bin width, a constant Gaussian energy resolution (FWHM in keV, per spectrum; 0 = none) and a log/linear axis can be set; the resolution used is printed in the plot and in the saved files. The energy range is selected by dragging with the mouse on the plot (double-click resets it); the saved pictures show the selected range and the visible curves, the ASCII and ROOT files the whole spectrum with one column or histogram per curve; the strongest lines are listed below the plot. Save all spectra as writes every spectrum of every section as PNG or PDF (one zip with a file per spectrum), as one ASCII file (lines and histograms) or as one ROOT file with TH1D histograms; each window can also save its own spectrum.

How the decay quantities are calculated. Activity A = N ln 2 / T½; the rate of each radiation is A times its number of particles per decay from the decay data (MF 8 / MT 457: for every spectrum the discrete part times the intensity, plus the continuum part). Beta⁻ spectra come from the end-point energies and intensities (or from the tabulated continuum when the evaluation has one) with the allowed shape and the relativistic Fermi function (no forbidden shape factors and no screening); beta⁺ spectra use the electron-capture / β⁺ lines: end-point = Q − 1021.997 keV and the β⁺ intensity is the part of the electron-capture / β⁺ branching that goes through positron emission. Alpha, gamma + X-ray (with the 511 keV annihilation photons listed in the data) and conversion + Auger electron lines are put into the energy bins and then folded with the Gaussian resolution (lines at their own energy, continua from the bin centres, area conserved, the part below zero energy is lost). Electron capture, conversion and Auger electrons, X rays and annihilation photons are not counted in the table totals, but they are in the spectra. The rates are emitted inside the material: nothing is absorbed, scattered or self-shielded, and the half-lives are those of the library.

Vacuum window. The first layer, in front of everything else, is a vacuum window. It is only used to attenuate the neutron fluence: no reaction rates are calculated for it, and everything behind it (rates, plots, the simulated experiment) uses the fluence that is transmitted through it. It is off until you switch it on (click it); double-click it to open its window. Material offers Mylar, beryllium, Kapton, aluminium and stainless steel 304 and 316L. When you select one, the window prints its chemical composition (mass % and atom % of every element), the natural isotopic abundances (NIST), the mean atomic mass, the handbook density and where the values come from (the steels are the middle of the ASTM A240 ranges, iron being the balance). The default thickness is 100 µm; thickness (µm) and density (g/cm³) can be changed, and Use handbook density restores the density.

Custom window material. Custom material at the end of the list lets you type your own: write the element symbols (capital first letter) with their number of atoms per formula unit, for example Si1 O2, C10H8O4 or Fe 0.7 Cr 0.19 Ni 0.11 (a missing number means 1), or choose Mass fractions and type those; give an optional name and the density (for a single element, such as Fe or Pb, the density of the pure element at about 20 °C is filled in automatically, for gases at 20 °C and 101.325 kPa; you can still overwrite it). Each element is taken with its natural isotopic composition and atomic masses (NIST; elements without a natural composition, such as Tc, are refused), and the composition table is printed. From then on the material is treated exactly like the pre-defined ones. Mistakes in the text are reported at once, and for a compound the window is not applied until a density is given.

Cross sections and temperature. The total cross section of the window is the sum of the total cross sections (MT 1) of its natural isotopes weighted with their atom fractions, taken from the loaded library. All isotopes use tables of the same temperature, the one in the name of the table (for example 294 K). Cross-section temperature is automatic (the temperature closest to 294 K for which every isotope has a table) or chosen from the list, which also says how many isotopes have a table at each temperature. The tables that were used are listed under the fields.

Air gap. Below the thickness and density you can enter the Air gap thickness in cm: the air between the vacuum window and the target (0 = none). The air is dry air at sea level (N₂ 78.084 %, O₂ 20.946 %, Ar 0.934 %, CO₂ 0.0412 % by volume), with its composition printed, and a density of 1.204 mg/cm³ (1.204 kg/m³ at 20 °C and 101.325 kPa); change the density for another pressure or temperature (it scales as pressure/temperature) and Use dry air, 20 °C restores it. N, O, Ar and C are used with their natural isotopes, and with the same temperature as the window.

Mixed temperatures. If some isotope of the window or of the air has no table at the chosen temperature, a framed warning names the isotopes, the share of atoms affected and the temperatures at which tables do exist. Until you press OK, proceed with mixed temperatures those isotopes are left out; after OK their tables at the closest temperature are used, the cross sections of the window and the air are then at different temperatures, and the warning stays with an Undo button (the acceptance is also written in the run information of the experiment). Changing the material, the temperature or switching the air gap on or off asks again. An isotope without any table is left out with a note.

Transmission plot. The window shows the transmission versus neutron energy. Without an air gap there is one curve (the window); with an air gap there are three: the transmission through the vacuum window, through the air, and the total through both. The vertical axis autoscales between 1 and the lowest transmission in the energy window (plus a small margin), and the values at 25.3 meV, 1 eV, 1 keV, 1 MeV and 14 MeV are listed under the fields. The rates use the total.

Sample, beam spot and energy window. The Beam spot radius is in the Target definition panel (it applies to all layers) and the energy window in the Energy window and self-shielding panel. Sample mass (mg) and Number of atoms N are linked: N is the number of atoms of the primary isotope (it can be real, for example 1.5e18), and the mass is the total mass of all components. The atoms of the other components are r × N, where r is the ratio you entered, and they are listed in Rates by reaction. N follows from the mass as N = mass / (AWR × 1.00866 u / NA) for a single isotope, and with the summed atomic masses of all components for a compound. Thickness, Density and Beam spot radius are also linked: the spot is taken as a circle of area A = π r², and thickness = mass / (density × A). Editing the thickness changes the mass. Density starts at the density of the element (a table inside the page); you can overwrite it, and Use element density restores the default. For a compound there is no default, so enter the density of the mixture. E min and E max set the energy window of the integral. Include self-shielding switches the correction on or off (see section 8).

Setup (JSON and link). The Setup group at the top of the left panel saves everything that defines a calculation: beam line, repetition rate and flight path, isotope with its table (ZAID and temperature) and ticked reactions, the components of every layer (with their ratios, reactions and layer), the mass and density of the front layer and the backing, the vacuum window (on or off, material, typed composition, temperature, thickness, density, air gap and an accepted mixed-temperature choice), the experiment-window settings (including the stacked targets counted and the sources included in the spectrum), a fluence table you loaded, the path of the cross-section library, sample mass, spot radius and density if you changed it, energy range, resolution settings, self-shielding model, rate or yield, per atom or per sample, axes and the curve shown. Save setup (JSON) writes it to a small file whose name contains the isotopes, the front layer and the backing (for example setup_Au-197_front-Fe-56_back-B-10_n_TOF-EAR1.json), and Load setup reads it back; setups saved by older versions are converted. Copy link puts the same setup in the address of the page (after #setup=) and copies it, so that a result can be shared by a link: whoever opens it gets the same settings and only has to load the cross-section library. The files do not contain the cross sections themselves. Library in Chrome and Edge: Choose folder… remembers the folder in this browser. When the page opens it reloads that folder by itself if the browser still allows it; otherwise a Reload saved library button appears (also next to Load setup): one click and Allow load the library and apply the isotopes of the setup. Choose Allow on every visit in the browser prompt to make it fully automatic. In other browsers, or on another computer, the setup holds only the path as a reminder, because a web page cannot open a folder from a path: choose the folder and the isotopes are applied. A fluence table you loaded yourself is included in the setup (and in the link, which then gets long). If the library is not loaded yet when a setup is read, the isotopes are applied as soon as it is; isotopes missing from the library are reported. In Chrome and Edge, Setups folder… chooses the folder where Save setup writes the JSON directly and where Load setup opens; the browser remembers it and asks permission once per visit. Copy link puts the whole setup (without the cross sections) into a web address and copies it, so that you can send it to a colleague or keep it in a note; opening that address in this page loads the setup, and the cross-section library has to be chosen again on that computer. Paste link loads a setup from such a link without opening it: press it, paste the link (or only the code after #setup=) into the box and press Enter. Use it when the address does not open, for example when the link was copied from an embedded view of the page. All links start with the same text; the part that differs is at the end, and the link box is cleared when a setup is loaded.

4. Reading the results (main panel)

Summary band. Rate per target atom (and the thin-sample value when self-shielding is on), rate in the sample, and reactions per beam pulse, for the reaction chosen in Results for.

Results for. This selector above the charts lists the ticked reactions of every component, grouped by isotope. An isotope with two or more ticked reactions also has Σ selected reactions, the sum of its ticked reactions. The choice drives the summary band, the cumulative curve, the per-atom curves and the facility comparison. For an extra isotope, the in-sample and per-pulse values use that isotope's number of atoms (its ratio × N).

Whole compound. With two or more isotopes that have reactions ticked, Results for also offers Σ over isotopes, weighted by atoms. The charts then show, for each isotope, the sum of its ticked reactions (the individual reaction curves are left out) multiplied by its atoms per primary atom, and the compound total as the main curve. The cross section is then in barn per primary atom, and the rate density is per primary atom or per sample, as set by the toggle. The summary band, the cumulative curve, the decade table and the facility comparison use the compound total. The self-shielding of the sample is already common to all isotopes.

Sum of reactions. Σ is built as one cross section σΣ(E) = Σ σk(E) on the union of the energy grids of its reactions, and is then folded with the fluence and the shielding like any other reaction. Because the shielding factor depends only on the total cross section, the rate density of the sum is exactly the sum of the individual rate densities. Overlapping channels (MT 101 with MT 102, MT 3 with MT 2 or MT 4, MT 1 with anything) are counted twice, so tick only channels that do not overlap.

Reaction rate or yield. Third plot swaps the third panel between the reaction rate density and the reaction yield, the number of reactions per incident neutron: Y(E) = (N / A) σeff(E), with N the number of atoms of the chosen isotope in the sample, A the beam spot area and σeff = rate density per atom / fluence (self-shielding and the resolution function included). For a thin sample this is (N / A) σ(E); for a thick one it tends to (1 − e−nσtot) σ / σtot. The cumulative curve and the per-atom / per-sample choice only apply to the rate. Saved plots and ROOT histograms follow the choice (the histogram is called yield).

Charts. Three panels share one logarithmic energy axis: the fluence Φ(E), the cross section σ(E), and the rate density (rate per unit ln E). With a vacuum window (and air gap) the fluence panel shows the fluence behind them. The energy values are written under every panel and, when you zoom into a narrow window, switch to round numbers on a linear scale so there is always a reference. Each panel has its title and units above it and, where needed, a colour legend in a band above the frame, so nothing is written over the curves. When several reactions are drawn, every ticked reaction of the chosen isotope has its own colour in the σ and rate-density panels (with Σ drawn first when it is the chosen result), and the cumulative curve belongs to the chosen result. Linear / Log sets the vertical axis of all three panels (fluence, cross section and rate density; log by default); on log axes the range grows up to 18 decades when several curves are drawn. Per atom / Per sample chooses whether the rate density is for one atom or for the whole sample (the default is the whole sample). The dashed black line is the cumulative rate in % (right axis); the dotted line is the thin-sample value, shown for a single curve when self-shielding is on. The shaded band marks energies below the pulse-overlap energy. The Axis toggle switches the horizontal axis between neutron energy and time of flight (see section 6). Move the pointer over the charts to read E, t and the values there; drag across the charts to zoom in; double-click or press Reset zoom to return to the full window.

Zooming. Drag across the plots to zoom the energy range only (the vertical axes stay as they are). Hold Shift while dragging to draw a box inside one plot: the horizontal range of all plots follows the width of the box and the vertical range of that plot follows its height. A Shift-drag that is nearly horizontal or nearly vertical (under 6 pixels in the other direction) changes only one axis. A plot with a manual vertical range shows [y zoomed] after its title; the other plots stay on autoscale, and the rate and the yield plots remember their ranges separately. Double-click a plot or press Reset zoom to return to the full energy range and autoscale in all plots. Save plot as PNG or PDF stores what is on screen, while ASCII and ROOT files hold the whole data.

Rate by energy decade. The share of the total rate in each decade of neutron energy, in %, in 1/s for the sample and per pulse. Its own Reaction selector, next to the title, offers the same list as Results for. Same as results (the default) follows that selector; any other choice keeps the decade table on that reaction or sum while the rest of the page shows something else.

Rates by reaction. Shown when more than one reaction or component is listed. It has one row for every ticked reaction of every component, with the atoms, the rate per atom, in the sample and per pulse. A component with no reaction ticked is listed as shielding only. The last row sums the listed reactions; overlapping channels are counted twice there.

All facilities, same isotope and reaction. A collapsed panel at the bottom: it is calculated only while it is open. The same calculation for every beam line, with the reaction chosen in Results for. Each line is at its nominal rate and path (your own settings for the selected line). The columns are the rate per atom, in the sample and per pulse. Relative is a bar for the per-atom rate on a logarithmic scale: the best facility is a full bar, and each factor of 10 below it shortens the bar by one sixth (minimum 2 %). Read the exact ratios from the numbers.

Saving the plots. The Save plot button (next to Reset zoom) opens a menu with PNG, PDF, ASCII and ROOT histograms. PNG and PDF save the three panels as they are shown, with a white background (the PDF is one page that holds the picture). ASCII writes one text table with a commented header (run description and a numbered list of columns). It always holds the full energy range, whatever the zoom of the plots, and both the neutron energy (MeV) and the time of flight (ns, for the flight path and the resolution function in use) in columns 1 and 2, then the fluence, the cross section, the reaction rate and the reaction yield of the main curve, of the sum of the selected reactions and of every individual reaction (MT) of every component, with the compound total if there is one. Rates and yields are always both written, whichever of the two is displayed (the page recomputes the yield, which always includes the attenuation in the sample, and restores the display). The rate is per atom or in the sample as set by the buttons; the yield is reactions per incident neutron. Curves with another energy grid are linearly interpolated onto the grid of column 1. ROOT histograms writes one ROOT file with the same curves as TH1D histograms over the full energy range (the zoom is not used), each one twice: against the energy (sigma, rate, yield, …) and against the time of flight in ns (the same names with the suffix _tof, values unchanged and bins in increasing time): phi, sigma, rate, yield, rate_thin_sample, rate_without_resolution, rate_cumulative_fraction and, when several reactions or components are selected, one histogram for every individual reaction and for each sum (names such as sigma_n_g_MT102, rate_n_2n_MT16, rate_sum_selected; the first histograms sigma and rate are the curve shown as main curve, usually the sum). Everything selected is written, whichever curve is displayed. Each bin spans the geometric mid-points between neighbouring points of the table (in MeV, or in ns for the _tof histograms), and its content is the value at that point (the rate density is per unit ln E). The file is delivered inside a zip because the viewer only allows some file types. You are asked to confirm the file name.

5. Experiment simulation (counts and transmission)

Window and settings. The Experiment simulation card opens a window (drag the title bar, resize at the corner, ✕ to close). Nothing is calculated until you press Run. It simulates a measurement with the isotopes, layers, fluence and resolution function set in the main page. The settings are collapsible (they fold away after the first run so the plots are in view) and grouped. Measurement: experiment type (counting, transmission or both), number of identical targets in the beam, measurement time and unit. Counts: which targets and layers: Stacked targets counted picks the positions of the stack that are summed (all, one, a list such as 1,3 or a range such as 2-4; target k sees the beam attenuated by the k−1 targets before it, and no interaction between targets is modelled), and three boxes include or exclude the counts of the samples, the front layer and the sample backing in the spectrum. All ticked reactions of every layer are counted (the choice under Results for in the main page does not apply). Output: energy or time of flight, logarithmic (bins per decade) or linear (number of bins) binning, and the range (default: the energy range of the selected facility fluence; a lower limit in time of flight acts as a cut of the gamma flash). Detector and background: detection efficiency (constant or a table of E and efficiency in %), flat background in counts/s per µs of time of flight, an optional uploaded background spectrum, and whether the background is measured for the same time or known exactly. Transmission: sample-in share of the beam time, detector efficiency and background. Statistics: expected values (±√N) or one simulated measurement (random seed), and an optional target relative uncertainty. Under the Run bar come the plots, then the summary with the run information.

Counting experiment. Counts per bin = rate in the bin × time × efficiency, for the samples, the front layer and the backing separately, plus the background. The Targets plotted pulldown above the plots chooses which of them (and which position in the stack, or several positions) are plotted; changing it re-runs the experiment with the same random seed, and the summary, the net signal and its uncertainty refer to that selection. For N identical targets the beam reaching target k is attenuated by the k−1 targets before it (first-collision model, factor Tk−1 with T = e−τ, τ the optical depth of one target unit, all layers); all positions give (1 − TN)/(1 − T). The vacuum window and the air gap are not part of this repeated target unit: they sit once in front of the first target and act once on the fluence, for every sample, the front layer, the backing and the open-beam run, and they are not repeated for the stacked targets. The summary gives, for each part, the counts and the expected count rate (counts/s), then the total of the selected parts plus background and the net signal. The plots show the counts of each part, the relative uncertainty of the net signal and, with a target uncertainty, the time needed for it (per bin and for the whole range). Counts per bin grow in proportion to the measurement time and the relative uncertainty falls as 1/√time.

Target precision. The optional Target relative uncertainty (%) gives the precision wanted per bin. As the relative uncertainty falls as 1/√t, the page reports the time needed, tneeded = t × (relative uncertainty achieved)² / target², for the net signal (counting) or for T (transmission): a plot, a column in the ASCII file, a ROOT histogram and, in the summary, the time for the integrated signal. Empty: nothing is calculated and no column is written.

Uncertainties, simulated measurement and net signal. With Expected values the error of a bin is √N. With One simulated measurement every part (samples, front layer, backing, background) is drawn from its own Poisson distribution (the seed makes it reproducible), shown as points with 68.27 % Garwood intervals together with the expected curves (dashed); the total is the sum of the draws. Legends: click a name in a plot legend to hide or show that curve; the vertical range follows the visible curves. The net signal is the total counts in the spectrum minus the background estimate, per bin and for the whole range. The background estimate is the flat and uploaded background plus the front-layer and backing counts when the samples are included, so the net signal is essentially the counts of the samples. With Measured for the same time the subtracted background is a second independent measurement (variance S + 2B for a signal S and background B); with Known exactly it is subtracted without noise (S + B). The relative uncertainty is √variance / S, and the time needed for a target uncertainty follows from it.

Transmission experiment and the uncertainty of the total cross section. Sample-in and sample-out (open beam) runs share the beam time (the sample-in share is set, and the best share is reported: √α/(√α+√β) with α and β the relative variances of the in and out runs per unit time). In each bin the rates reaching the detector are rin (resolution-folded fluence × e−Nτ × detector efficiency) and rout (open beam). The background is a fixed fraction f of the open-beam rate, b = f rout, in both runs. The expected gross counts are Ein = (rin + b) tin and Eout = (rout + b) tout; Expected values uses them directly and a simulated measurement replaces them by independent Poisson draws kin and kout. The net counts are nin = kin − b tin and nout = kout − b tout (the background is taken as known and subtracted without noise). Then T = nin / nout with relative variance rv = kin/nin² + kout/nout² (Poisson errors of the gross counts; the two runs are independent, so there is no covariance term), so σT = T √rv. The total cross section per primary atom is σ = −ln T / (N nP), where nP is the number of primary atoms per barn (atoms / beam area × 10⁻²⁴) and N the number of stacked targets; for a compound this is the total cross section per primary atom. Its uncertainty follows from dσ = dT / (T N nP), that is σerr = √rv / (N nP). The plot shows σ with ±1σ; the expected curve is σ from the unperturbed T. Limits: only counting statistics are included (no uncertainty on the background level, the efficiency, the thickness or the fluence); the propagation is first order and symmetric (the Garwood intervals are shown on the count points only); in a bin where nin or nout is zero or negative T and σ are left blank; the bins are independent and the resolution is not unfolded; transmission is pure attenuation, e−Nτ, and scattered neutrons that still reach the detector are not modelled. Counting and transmission runs both, the counting detector counting during the sample-in time.

Run information, plots and files. The summary, the plot header, the ASCII file and the ROOT histograms describe the beam spot radius, the vacuum window and the air gap (material, thickness, density, temperature, and a note if mixed temperatures were accepted), and the composition, mass, density and thickness of the sample, front layer and backing, with the total target mass; the information is fixed when you press Run. The x and y ranges of the plots are independent of the main page (drag: x range; Shift-drag: box); tick Link x range to make them follow the main plots. Save plot gives PNG, PDF, ASCII and ROOT (histograms with bin errors, and the counts of every part). The settings are part of the saved setup and link, and the setup file name includes the front layer and the backing. Limits of the simulator: counting statistics only, with no dead time or pile-up, no gaps between stacked targets and no neutrons scattered from one target into the next.

6. Resolution and time of flight

Resolution (time of flight). Tick Apply resolution function. The resolution width is the quadrature sum σ = √(σpulse² + σadd² + σtarget(E)²) of the particle pulse width of the beam line (with its default value), an additional width and the energy-dependent target / moderator spread (a fixed path length ΔL, or the Monte Carlo resolution function). The pulse and additional widths are entered as FWHM (σ = FWHM / 2.3548) or as σ; the target spread is a FWHM. The flight path is the one in the name of the beam line, in metres (for example 185 m for n_TOF-EAR1), not the value in the Flight path field. The hint below the field gives the corresponding energy resolution ΔE/E at 1 eV, 1 keV and 1 MeV for that beam line.

Every energy is converted to a time of flight with the relativistic relation t = L / (β c), β = √(γ² − 1) / γ, γ = 1 + E / (mnc²), with mnc² = 939.565 MeV. The resolution is a Gaussian in t whose width depends on the neutron energy: σt(E)² = σpulse² + σadd² + σtarget(E)². The target part is a fixed path length, σtarget = ΔL / v(E) / 2.3548 (or the Monte Carlo resolution function, see above). For a fixed path length ΔE/E ≈ 2ΔL/L does not depend on energy. The cross section is not broadened: it stays the evaluated σ(E), because the resolution belongs to the measurement and not to the nucleus. Only the rate spectrum is blurred. The rate spectrum (after self-shielding) is treated as a count rate per ns, R(t) = ρ(E) / |dt/dlnE|. Each true energy spreads its counts with its own width, and the result is converted back, so the integral over the window is conserved apart from the window edges. This is the order in which a real measurement works: the sample shields with the true cross section and the detector then blurs the spectrum. The fluence is not broadened. With a single curve and the resolution on, the rate-density panel also draws the unresolved curve as a grey dashed line (without resolution), so you can see what the resolution does. The total rate is conserved by construction, so the numbers in the summary change very little; the effect is in the shape of narrow structures. The summary, the decade table, the cumulative curve and the facility comparison (each beam line with its own flight path) all use the resolved spectra. For widths smaller than the energy grid spacing nothing changes.

Axis. The Axis toggle above the charts switches the horizontal axis between neutron energy and time of flight (logarithmic, with the shortest times on the left, so high energies are still on the left). Zooming, the pointer readout (which shows both E and t) and the pulse-overlap shading work in both modes.

7. How the rate is calculated

For one atom, R = ∫ σ(E) Φ(E) d lnE, with σ in barn (1 b = 10⁻²⁴ cm²) and Φ in n/(cm²·s) per unit ln E. The sample rate is R × (number of atoms), and the rate per pulse is that value divided by the repetition rate.

The integration grid is the union of the ACE energies and the fluence energies inside the window, refined so that no step exceeds Δ ln E = 0.03 (3 %). The cross section is interpolated linearly in energy (the ACE convention), Φ log–log (linear if it contains zeros), and the integral is a trapezoid sum in ln E. The cumulative curve, the decade shares and the total all come from the same sum.

Changing the setup scales the fluence: Φ ∝ (f / f₀) (L₀ / L)², where f₀ and L₀ are the nominal values. The fluence is time-averaged at the nominal rate, so "1/s" means reactions per second of beam time for every facility. The instantaneous rate during a pulse is higher by the inverse duty cycle. The 1/L² law assumes a point-like source seen from far away.

Vacuum window and air gap. The window and the air gap are not part of the sample. Their optical depth is τw(E) = nw Σi fi σtot,i(E) for the window and likewise τa(E) for the air, with n = ρ t NA / Ā the atoms per barn (density ρ, thickness t, mean atomic mass Ā of the material in g/mol), fi the atom fraction of every natural isotope (elements: mass or atom fractions converted with the NIST atomic masses, isotopes: NIST abundances) and σtot,i its MT 1 cross section at one common temperature. The cross sections of all isotopes are merged (interpolated linearly in energy) and evaluated at the energy points (bins) of the neutron spectrum of the selected facility, so the transmissions plotted and used share the binning of the spectrum (without a spectrum a thinned cross-section grid is used instead). The fluence entering the rate integral is Φ(E) e−(τw+τa). This holds whether or not Include self-shielding is ticked, because it acts on the fluence, not on the sample. Beam hardening is thus included, scattering back into the beam is not (first-collision attenuation).

Sample and atoms. The number of atoms in the beam is N = mass / (AWR × 1.00866 u). The sample is a uniform slab of areal density n = N / (π r²), uniformly illuminated by the beam. The density only converts between mass and thickness (thickness = mass / (density × area)); it starts at the density of the element of the selected isotope and you can overwrite it. Editing the thickness changes the mass.

Compound. The isotope in the Sample panel is the primary component and N is its number of atoms. Each extra component i has ri atoms per primary atom, so its areal density is ri n. The shielding weight uses τ = Σi ri n σtot,i(E), summed over all components, so a scatterer such as oxygen shields the resonances of uranium. The sample mass is the total mass N Σ ri Ai u. The element density no longer applies, so enter the density of the mixture to get a thickness.

ACE input. ASCII type-1 continuous-energy neutron tables, located through the xsdir. Isotopes are identified by their ZAID string (for example 26056.80c). Reactions come from the MTR/SIG blocks, plus total (MT 1), elastic (MT 2) and absorption (MT 101) from the main grid. MT 3 is derived as total minus elastic. The cross section is never broadened by the program.

Pulse overlap: neutrons slower than E = ½ mn (L f / c)² arrive after the next pulse has started, so below that energy the spectrum mixes several pulses.

8. Self-shielding

A strong resonance absorbs most of the beam in the first layers, so the real rate is lower than the thin-sample value. The page treats the sample as a uniform slab of areal density n = N / area (atoms per barn) and multiplies the integrand by (1 − e−τ) / τ, with τ(E) = Σi ri n σtot,i(E). The sum runs over the primary isotope (r = 1) and all components, using MT 1 of each table. This is the first-collision attenuation of the beam. The "peak optical depth" is the largest τ in the window; the thin-sample percentage shows how much the correction lowers the total.

Self-shielding model. Three models are available in the sample section. (1) Simple: first collision is the factor above; a neutron that scatters is lost. (2) Second collision (analytic) adds the neutrons that scatter once and then react: for an infinite slab, normal incidence and isotropic scattering without energy change, Y2 = (Σel/Σt)(Σx/Σt) G(T), with G(T) = ∫0T e−t[1 − E2(t)/2 − E2(T−t)/2] dt, T = n Σt, and Σel from MT 2. (3) Multiple scattering (Monte Carlo) follows the neutrons after the first collision (the first collision itself is exact and analytic): elastic scattering, isotropic in the centre of mass, with the energy change, a free-gas target below 400 kT at the temperature of the table, and every other reaction removing the neutron. It runs in the background on a grid of energies refined where the total cross section changes (histories per energy are set in the box; 2000 is a good start), and the plots update when it is ready. For thin samples the three models agree. They differ at strong resonances of thick samples, and the Monte Carlo also follows the energy change in a scattering, which matters when the energy loss is comparable to the resonance width. The yield and the rate saved in the files and the plots use the chosen model.

Limits of the models: the simple model treats scattered neutrons as removed (no scatter-in), so it is a lower bound of the rate for scattering-dominated samples. The correction models assume an infinite slab of the areal density N / area (no lateral leakage), elastic scattering from MT 2 only (no inelastic scatter-in, no angular distributions from the ACE file), no delay of the scattered neutron in the time of flight, and no interaction between several targets in a stack. The beam is assumed uniform over a circular spot of area π r². There is no Doppler or sample-temperature treatment beyond the temperature of the table you choose, and unresolved-resonance probability tables are not used. Cross sections are those of the pointwise ACE table.

9. Tips and checks

If the rate looks too low in a resonance, lower the mass or enlarge the spot radius, and compare with the dotted thin-sample line. A very small E min needs a fluence table that reaches it; outside the table the fluence is not extrapolated, so the rate there is zero. Check the status line below the folder button when a library does not load: it reports the file name and the problem. The resolution only changes structures that are not much wider than it. In the resonance region, check the ΔE/E in the hint against the resonance widths: a resolution much narrower than a resonance leaves it unchanged, while a much wider one flattens it. In time-of-flight mode the dashed cumulative curve still accumulates in energy, so it falls from left to right. If an extra isotope shows no rate, make sure at least one reaction is ticked for it; with none ticked it only shields. In the experiment window, counts per bin grow in proportion to the measurement time and the relative uncertainty falls as 1/√time; a layer that is switched off or unticked adds nothing to the spectrum.

Background file (energy, value, uncertainty)
Info
Efficiency: fraction of the reactions that is detected (counts include it); a table of efficiency against energy replaces the constant. Flat background: counts per second per µs of time of flight, constant; an uploaded spectrum (ASCII file with lines x value uncertainty: x as energy in MeV or time of flight in µs according to the selector, value and 1σ uncertainty in counts/s per µs of TOF; the uncertainty column is optional, but then every line needs it; # starts a comment) is added to it. Normalised: the background rate (flat value and uploaded spectrum, counts/s per µs of TOF) is that of a separate background run of duration tb (Background measuring time; empty means the same as the sample run). The normalisation factor f scales the rate to the conditions of the sample run, for example the ratio of the beam intensities (it does not include the time ratio), so the background added to the spectrum and subtracted is f × rate × measuring time of the sample run. The subtraction is estimated from the Nb counts of the background run, which has the weight f·t/tb, so its variance is (f·t/tb)² Nb: a longer background run reduces the uncertainty. If the uploaded file has an uncertainty column, that part of the background is subtracted with the uncertainty of the file (σ per bin, scaled by the time and by f) instead of the Poisson uncertainty of its counts; the flat part keeps the Poisson one, and the bins are taken as independent. Subtraction: Measured means that the background is measured for the same time as the sample, so its statistical uncertainty adds to the net signal; Known exactly subtracts it without uncertainty.
